# Measles vaccination gap: problem definition before ideation

**Prepared:** 15 October 2026  
**Evidence period:** 2019–2025; latest WHO/UNICEF estimates released July 2026  
**Stage:** Global and multi-country problem discovery, with district analysis where reported data are available.

The [completed 2025 global screen](07_global_country_and_district_screen_2025.md) compares every country and WHO region and audits public country-level district summaries.

## 1. Decision this brief supports

Compare **children missing routine measles vaccination** across WHO regions and countries, identify areas for deeper district analysis, and match solution options to the barriers found in those areas. The likely users are national and local immunization programmes and partners that can help deliver or evaluate a response. A global comparison can begin now; implementation choices require local evidence and ownership.

## 2. Complete problem statement

In 2025, nearly **21 million children worldwide missed the routine first dose** of measles vaccine. WHO/UNICEF estimate that **84%** of children received the first dose (MCV1) and **77%** received the second dose (MCV2); MCV1 coverage was still below its 2019 level of 86%. The gap is uneven: in low-income countries, estimated 2025 coverage was **69% for MCV1** and **57% for MCV2**. WHO recommends sustained coverage of **at least 95% with each dose in every community** to prevent outbreaks. Measles is highly contagious and can cause severe illness and death; **57 countries** reported large or disruptive outbreaks in 2025. The problem for a health team is that too many eligible children are not receiving both scheduled doses, and national averages conceal where the missed children are. The specific local barriers, number of affected children, and appropriate response remain to be established before ideas are chosen. [WHO vaccination overview](https://www.who.int/en/health-topics/vaccines-and-immunization); [WHO/UNICEF 2025 release](https://www.who.int/news/item/15-07-2026-global-childhood-immunization-coverage-inches-forward-despite-conflict-and-hesitancy---unicef--who); [WHO measles guidance](https://www.who.int/emergencies/disease-outbreak-news/item/2025-DON561)

## 3. Who, where, when, and why it matters

| Element | Current definition |
| --- | --- |
| Affected people | Children due for MCV1 or MCV2 under their country's schedule; caregivers bear the effort of accessing and returning to services. Unvaccinated young children face the greatest risk of severe complications. |
| Place | A global problem, with especially low reported coverage in low-income countries and the WHO African Region. Compare all reporting countries and regions, then examine districts where suitable data exist. |
| Time | 2025 is the most recent annual coverage baseline in this brief; 2019 is the pre-pandemic comparison. Local programmes need a more frequent operating view. |
| Healthcare consequence | Gaps in protection permit outbreaks and can lead to severe illness, deaths, and pressure on clinical and public-health services. WHO estimated 95,000 measles deaths worldwide in 2024; that is a different year and is not an estimate of deaths caused by the 2025 coverage gap. |
| Responsible actors | National and local immunization programmes own vaccine delivery and records; community health workers, clinics, caregivers, and surveillance teams hold information needed to understand the gap. Roles vary by country. |

[WHO measles fact sheet](https://www.who.int/en/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/measles)

## 4. What the available data actually show

Percent of the relevant target population covered, from the downloaded 2025 WHO GHO indicators:

| Geography or group | MCV1 2019 | MCV1 2025 | MCV2 2019 | MCV2 2025 |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| Global | 86% | 84% | 71% | 77% |
| WHO African Region | 72% | 71% | 33% | 58% |
| Low-income countries | 71% | 69% | 34% | 57% |
| High-income countries | 94% | 93% | 93% | 91% |

The 2025 first-dose gap between low- and high-income groups is **24 percentage points**; for the second dose it is **34 points**. MCV2 improved substantially from 2019 in low-income countries and the African Region, but remained far below the 95% benchmark. In the 2025 MCV1 file, **49 of 195 countries** have coverage below 80%. This country count is unweighted and cannot be translated into a count of missed children.

### Geographic coverage and comparability

| Level | What can be analyzed now | Limit |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Global and six WHO regions | MCV1 and MCV2 estimates, 2019–2025 | Regional averages can conceal country and district gaps. |
| All 195 countries in the downloaded WHO/UNICEF estimates | Annual national MCV1 and MCV2 trends and comparisons | Estimates mix reporting and extrapolation; second-dose ages and vaccine introduction follow local schedules, so check country context before ranking MCV2 values. |
| Subnational areas | WHO says 147 Member States shared some 2025 admin1 or admin2 data for DTP1, DTP3, and/or MCV1, covering more than 29,000 reported areas. The public System performance portal also provides country-level district coverage-band summaries for MCV1 and MCV2. | The detailed named-area coverage dataset is unpublished and requires a data request. The public summaries do not identify individual districts. Admin1 and admin2 are different geographic levels, and reported administrative rates need denominator checks. |

The defensible scope is **all-country screening plus public district-band summaries**, with coverage gaps clearly labelled. Named-district analysis can follow when detailed WHO or national data are available. A complete, comparable MCV1-and-MCV2 dataset for every district in every country is not established by these WHO sources. [WHO/UNICEF national estimates](https://www.who.int/news-room/questions-and-answers/item/who-unicef-estimates-of-national-immunization-coverage); [WHO subnational coverage](https://www.who.int/teams/immunization-vaccines-and-biologicals/Immunization-data-and-decision-making/global-monitoring/immunization-coverage/subnational-immunization-coverage-data); [WHO data-access note](https://immunizationdata.who.int/docs/librariesprovider21/additional-datasets/how-to-access-more-data.pdf?sfvrsn=ee878941_1)

**Interpretation limit:** MCV1 and MCV2 are measured against their respective target populations and locally recommended ages, which can differ. Subtracting their percentages does **not** yield a valid individual dropout rate. Check national schedule and vaccine-introduction status before treating a zero MCV2 estimate as service failure. WHO/UNICEF separately estimate that 7.3 million infants received DTP1 but missed MCV1; that is a different, cohort-based measure. [WHO/UNICEF 2025 release](https://www.who.int/news/item/15-07-2026-global-childhood-immunization-coverage-inches-forward-despite-conflict-and-hesitancy---unicef--who); [WHO immunization coverage fact sheet](https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/immunization-coverage)

## 5. Boundary for the first brainstorm

**In scope:** Compare national MCV1 and MCV2 estimates across all available countries and regions; analyze public country-level district coverage bands where reported; identify missed groups and test explanations in selected local areas after obtaining detailed data. Measure intervention outcomes using each country's schedule and validated local denominators.

**To decide before implementation-focused ideation:** Which countries and local areas are candidates for deeper work, who owns each programme, the target ages, and whether the first focus is children who have never received MCV1, children overdue for MCV2, or both. These groups can require different operational responses.

**Outside the initial problem statement:** Treating measles cases, forecasting an outbreak from national coverage alone, and asserting that any one barrier explains low coverage everywhere. Outbreak response and surveillance remain important context, but they require separate local evidence and ownership.

## 6. What is known about possible causes

WHO/UNICEF describe several **possible** contributors across settings: conflict and displacement, limited access to services, disrupted supply, underfunding, and misinformation. None has been shown to be the leading cause in an unselected district. The brainstorm should use them as hypotheses for discovery, not as established diagnoses. [WHO/UNICEF 2025 release](https://www.who.int/news/item/15-07-2026-global-childhood-immunization-coverage-inches-forward-despite-conflict-and-hesitancy---unicef--who); [WHO/UNICEF 2024 release](https://www.who.int/news/item/15-07-2025-global-childhood-vaccination-coverage-holds-steady-yet-over-14-million-infants-remain-unvaccinated-who-unicef)

Questions that distinguish these mechanisms locally:

1. **Location and scale:** Which districts and communities have the largest *number* of missed children, and which have the lowest coverage? Both measures matter.
2. **Point of loss:** Are children never reached for MCV1, late for it, or missing MCV2 after receiving MCV1? What are the local schedule and eligibility rules?
3. **Service delivery:** Are vaccine stock, staffed sessions, opening hours, travel time, cost to families, and record transfer adequate where children are missed?
4. **Caregiver experience:** What do caregivers and frontline staff report about awareness, trust, convenience, safety concerns, and reasons for missed visits?
5. **Data validity:** Are target population estimates, doses administered, private-sector reports, migration, and duplicate records sufficiently accurate to locate the gap?
6. **Disease pattern:** Do case and outbreak surveillance data align with low-coverage places, allowing for reporting delays and under-ascertainment?

## 7. Solution options to evaluate

These are candidate approaches, **not prescriptions for an unselected country**. More than one may be needed. Select and adapt them with the responsible immunization programme after checking local barriers, vaccine policy, resources, and data quality.

| Option | Best fit if local evidence shows... | What a programme could test | Main tradeoff or requirement | Early signal of progress |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **A. Make routine services easier and more reliable to use** | Long travel or waiting times, inconvenient sessions, cancelled clinics, stockouts, or underserved settlements | Facility and district microplans; revised session times or sites; outreach to mapped underserved areas; reliable vaccine and staff availability | Requires staff, supply, cold chain, transport, and continuing funding; extra sessions do not help if missed families do not know or trust them | Sessions delivered as planned; stockout days; eligible children vaccinated in the target area |
| **B. Find and follow up children overdue for a dose** | Many children are recorded for MCV1 but later miss an age-eligible dose, or the local register can identify overdue children | Check due lists against vaccination records; offer appointments, reminders, or community health worker follow-up under local consent and privacy rules | Weak records, migration, or duplicate entries can exclude the very children most at risk; reminders cannot fix absent services | Verified overdue children contacted and vaccinated; fewer children remain overdue at the next review |
| **C. Run a targeted catch-up or supplementary campaign** | A sizeable immunity gap has accumulated in a defined place or age group, or outbreak risk requires faster reach | Programme-led campaign using WHO guidance to choose areas, age groups, delivery sites, and coverage checks | Needs policy approval, vaccine supply, staffing, funding, and careful eligibility rules; a campaign does not replace reliable routine services | Valid doses delivered to previously missed children; post-campaign coverage and remaining immunity gaps |
| **D. Co-design access and communication with communities** | Caregiver interviews show practical barriers, poor service experience, uncertainty, or low trust | Listen to caregivers and frontline workers; adapt clinic flow, invitation language, community links, and trusted messengers to the actual barriers | Generic awareness messaging may fail when transport, cost, or service quality is the binding constraint; effects must be evaluated | Fewer reported barriers and missed visits; improved uptake in the specific community |

**Foundational work across all options:** Use validated district coverage, child counts, service data, and measles surveillance to prioritize areas and monitor equity. Better analytics helps direct action but does not vaccinate children by itself. WHO's district microplanning guidance covers local maps, session plans, supplies, community links, and follow-up. Its campaign guidance describes targeted and selective campaigns; its demand guidance emphasizes diagnosing the actual reasons for low uptake before tailoring action. [WHO district microplanning](https://qualityhealthservices.who.int/quality-toolkit/qt-catalog-item/microplanning-for-immunization-service-delivery-using-the-reaching-every-district-%28-red%29-strategy); [WHO campaign guidance](https://www.who.int/publications/i/item/9789240103399); [WHO demand and uptake guidance](https://www.who.int/teams/immunization-vaccines-and-biologicals/essential-programme-on-immunization/demand)

**Selection rule:** If children cannot reach an available service, start with A. If a reliable register shows missed return visits while services are available, test B. If a mapped immunity gap needs rapid closure, assess C with the programme owner. If caregivers describe trust or experience barriers, pair the relevant service fix with D. Confirm baseline, feasibility, and equity before scaling any option. These conditions are hypotheses until a locality is chosen.

## 8. Measurement plan for the eventual local initiative

| Measure | Working definition | Why it matters / caution |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Primary: MCV1 coverage** | Children in the local MCV1 target cohort with a documented valid first dose, divided by eligible children; use the local schedule and reporting period. | Shows reach. Administrative doses divided by an estimated target population may misstate coverage and can exceed 100%; validate against registry or survey data when possible. |
| **Primary: MCV2 coverage** | Children in the local MCV2 target cohort with a documented valid second dose, divided by eligible children; use the locally recommended age. | Shows completion. Its denominator and age may differ from MCV1, so do not subtract the two rates to infer dropout. |
| **Diagnostic: missed children** | Count of eligible children overdue for each dose in a defined cohort and area, with unknown vaccination status shown separately. | Connects rates to the size of the need and to service planning. Requires a credible denominator or person-level registry. |
| **Guardrail: equity and data quality** | Coverage and missed-child counts by district and relevant groups, alongside missing or unverified records and denominator checks. | Prevents overall improvement from hiding unreached communities or apparent gains caused by poor records. |

**Benchmark:** WHO recommends sustained coverage of at least 95% with **both doses in every community**. This is a public-health benchmark, not a realistic short-term pilot target by itself. Set a time-bound local improvement target only after validating baseline, cohort size, resources, and measurement cadence. Measles cases and outbreaks are important lagging outcomes but are too rare and delayed to be the sole short-term pilot metric. [WHO measles guidance](https://www.who.int/emergencies/disease-outbreak-news/item/2025-DON561)

## 9. Evidence needed before choosing an option

| Required input | Why it changes the brainstorm | Likely source or owner |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Country, district, target ages, local vaccine schedule, programme owner | Defines who can act and which children count as overdue | National or district immunization programme |
| District MCV1/MCV2 numerators, denominators, recent trend, and missed-child counts | Distinguishes low percentages from large numbers of missed children | WHO subnational coverage where available; local registry or Joint Reporting Form |
| Vaccination records and data-quality assessment | Tests whether gaps reflect missed doses or recording/denominator errors | Clinics, immunization registry, coverage survey |
| Measles case and outbreak surveillance | Locates observed disease burden and monitors risk | Public-health surveillance team |
| Service availability and caregiver/frontline input | Identifies barriers that a local programme can actually address | Supply and staffing records; short interviews or field research |
| Available budget, staff, logistics, and legal/data constraints | Defines feasible and measurable responses | Programme owner and partners |

WHO reports that **147 Member States shared 2025 subnational immunization data**, but country and indicator coverage varies. National averages can hide local inequalities, and WHO's subnational data require careful denominator checks. [WHO subnational data](https://www.who.int/teams/immunization-vaccines-and-biologicals/Immunization-data-and-decision-making/global-monitoring/immunization-coverage/subnational-immunization-coverage-data); [WHO/UNICEF estimate limitations](https://www.who.int/news-room/questions-and-answers/item/who-unicef-estimates-of-national-immunization-coverage)

## 10. Ready-to-brainstorm gate

The team can compare the four options across all countries now. To brainstorm implementable versions in particular places, first confirm:

- the candidate countries and local areas, an accountable programme owner for each implementation area, and the eligible child cohorts;
- a credible local baseline for both doses and the number of children missed;
- the dominant barrier or a short, testable set of competing barrier hypotheses;
- available operating constraints and a feasible measurement window.

Until those are known, the solution-neutral prompt is: **How might immunization programmes identify and close measles vaccination gaps across countries while adapting delivery to the communities with the greatest unmet need?**
